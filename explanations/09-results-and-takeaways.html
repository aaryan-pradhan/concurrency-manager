1. How every number here was produced

Measurement conditions · read before quoting anything
  • Machine: Apple M3, 8 logical cores, macOS (Darwin 25.6), Apple clang 17, -std=c++23.
  • Pre-fix audit (2026-09-11): the text harness on a scratch copy with only the member-declaration order fixed, -O0 unless marked, 2 runs per file. The serial numbers join each thread right after starting it.
  • Post-fix benchmark (2026-09-11, evening): tests/benchmark.cpp at -O2 on the fix branch (binaries in the repo root are dated 22:48–22:50), 5 runs per point, detection interval 10 ms. The branch was merged to main as 47e3a19 the next morning. Configs A and B are in benchmark_results/results.csv. Config C's rows never reached the CSV: the sweep was killed during its 1,000-thread point, before the buffered file was flushed. Its 1–64-thread figures come from the sweep's printed summary, and its abort counts from its engine log.
  • Reproduction of the race: an instrumented copy in an earlier session's scratchpad, config B at 1,000 threads, 20 runs each with and without the skip fix.
  • Nothing was re-measured after the merge. The text harness hasn't been run on the fixed engine.

3. Speedup curves

\[ S(T) = \frac{\text{median throughput at } T \text{ threads}}{\text{median throughput at 1 thread}}, \qquad E(T) = \frac{S(T)}{T} \]

\(S\) is speedup and \(E\) is parallel efficiency: 1.0 means every added thread contributed fully.

Worked example · efficiency

B: \(E(8) = 7.97/8 = 0.996\), \(E(64) = 53.74/64 = 0.840\), and \(E(1000) = 13.98/1000 = 0.014\). C: \(E(4) = 2.83/4 = 0.708\) and \(E(64) = 0.25/64 = 0.0039\). A: \(E(32) = 0.24/32 = 0.0075\).

4. Why each curve looks like that

  • A, no work per operation: a transaction is a few lock-manager calls, microseconds of CPU, all serialized on one global mutex (Part 2, section 1). Extra threads add nothing to share out, only mutex contention, condition-variable wakeups and context switches, so throughput falls as threads are added. This is the curve that measures the lock manager itself, and it says the lock manager doesn't scale.
  • B, 1 ms per operation, rare conflicts: each transaction spends about 4 ms asleep while holding its locks, and two transactions share an item only about 1.6% of the time (Part 4, section 8). Threads sleep in parallel, so throughput tracks the ideal line up to 8 threads (99.6% efficient) and stays at 84% at 64. Beyond the 8 cores this still works, because a sleeping thread doesn't need a core.
  • B at 1,000 threads: 1,000 threads for 1,000 transactions means each thread runs about one transaction. The run is dominated by waking 1,000 threads, scheduling them on 8 cores, and 45 aborts. Throughput drops to 13.98×, below 16 threads, and 2 of 5 runs broke serializability (section 5).
  • C, 1 ms per operation, 20 items: overlap is about 59%, so waits chain and deadlocks multiply with threads. Each abort throws away up to 4 ms of work and adds backoff. Speedup peaks at 2.83× with 4 threads and collapses to 0.25× at 64, with 3,844 aborts for 1,000 commits. That's thrashing (page 4, section 10): past a point, adding concurrency reduces useful work.

5. Isolation

Result · serializability evidence
  • Pre-fix engine: 0 incompatible locks ever held together, but 4 committed upgraders per test.txt run were overtaken in the upgrade gap, each forming a precedence-graph cycle (Part 2, section 6).
  • Post-fix, 64 threads or fewer: 100 of 100 benchmark runs passed both checks (A: 30, B: 35, C: 35), plus the 6 quick-check runs and the 16 regression checks.
  • Post-fix, 1,000 threads: config B failed the serializability check in 2 of 5 runs (3 and 101 unsortable transactions). The lost-update check passed in all 5.
  • Race reproduction: 4 of 20 runs failed with the repo's behaviour, and 0 of 20 with the detector skipping releases that target a newer attempt (Part 3, section 6).

So the upgrade fix restored serializable behaviour in every recorded run up to 64 threads. At 1,000 threads a separate, identified race still produces non-serializable committed schedules, and its fix isn't merged.

6. Commit rate and the cost of aborts

Every benchmark run that finished committed every transaction, because a run can't finish otherwise: each worker retries until its commit succeeds. So "all transactions committed" is guaranteed by the retry loop. It's not a measure of the engine. The informative numbers are what it cost:

\[ \text{abort share} = \frac{\text{aborts}}{\text{aborts} + \text{commits}} \]

The fraction of all attempts that were thrown away. The text harness's "success rate" is \(1 -\) this (Part 4, section 5).

Worked example

C at 4 threads: \(23 / (23 + 1000) = 2.2\%\). C at 64 threads: \(3{,}844 / (3{,}844 + 1{,}000) = 3{,}844 / 4{,}844 = 79.4\%\), so four out of five attempts were wasted. B at 1,000 threads: \(45 / 1{,}045 = 4.3\%\). The pre-fix text harness on dead_test.txt: \(6{,}701 / 7{,}701 = 87.0\%\), which is the 13.0% "success rate" seen from the other side.

Config C at 1,000 threads is the limit of this approach. Run 0 took about 6 hours to commit 1,000 transactions, and one transaction was aborted 1,980 times (Part 3, section 7).

9. How much noise?

Worked example · spread between identical runs, (max − min) ÷ median

B at 1 thread: \((191.53 - 190.61)/191.15 = 0.5\%\). B at 1,000 threads: \((3{,}004.45 - 2{,}220.29)/2{,}671.77 = 29.3\%\). C at 64 threads: \((52.6 - 46.2)/47.9 = 13.4\%\). A at 1 thread: \((374{,}803.8 - 263{,}430.9)/333{,}625.3 = 33.4\%\), and those five runs rise steadily (263k, 306k, 334k, 359k, 375k), which looks like warm-up rather than random noise. That's why A's 1-thread baseline is the least trustworthy number on this page. Its ratios are still far below 1× even against its slowest run: \(79{,}181 / 263{,}431 = 0.30\) at 32 threads.

For correctness failures, the samples are tiny, so quote counts, not rates. A 95% Wilson interval shows how wide they are:

\[ \frac{\hat p + \frac{z^2}{2n}}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n}} \;\pm\; \frac{z}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n}} \sqrt{\frac{\hat p (1 - \hat p)}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}} \]

\(\hat p\) is the observed failure fraction, \(n\) the number of runs, and \(z = 1.96\) for 95%.

Worked example · 4 of 20 vs 0 of 20

\(\hat p = 0.2\), \(n = 20\): \(z^2 = 3.8416\), center \(= (0.2 + 0.09604)/1.19208 = 0.2483\), half-width \(= (1.96/1.19208)\sqrt{0.008 + 0.002401} = 1.6442 \times 0.10198 = 0.1677\). Interval: 8.1% to 41.6%. For 0 of 20: center \(= 0.09604/1.19208 = 0.0806\), half-width \(= 1.6442 \times \sqrt{0.002401} = 1.6442 \times 0.049 = 0.0806\). Interval: 0% to 16.1%. The intervals overlap. A one-sided Fisher exact test gives \(\binom{20}{4}\binom{20}{0}/\binom{40}{4} = 4{,}845/91{,}390 = 0.053\). The counts alone are suggestive, not conclusive. The stronger evidence is the mechanism: the instrumentation saw stale releases in the failing runs (Part 3, section 6).

10. What the evidence supports, and what it doesn't

Supported

  • A C++ lock manager with shared/exclusive locks, upgrades that keep S, and condition-variable waiting, run as rigorous ("strict") 2PL on every measured workload (Part 1, section 6).
  • Background wait-for-graph deadlock detection from a single-instant snapshot, DFS, victims chosen within the cycle, and restart with priority + 1 and jittered exponential backoff. Every completed run committed every transaction (Part 3, section 9).
  • With 1 ms of simulated I/O per operation and about 1.6% overlap: 7.97× at 8 threads and 53.74× at 64 threads over the same binary's serial run, with every run passing both checks (config B).
  • 1,000 concurrent threads, in both harnesses.
  • Correctness tooling: 16 regression checks and per-run precedence-graph and lost-update checks.

Not supported, or contradicted

  • ACID: no durability, and no stored data in the engine (Part 1, section 8).
  • Serializability guaranteed at 1,000 threads: contradicted, 2 of 5 and 4 of 20 runs (section 5).
  • A speedup without conditions: 0.24× on A at 32 threads and 0.25× on C at 64. The speedup depends entirely on work per operation, contention and thread count.
  • A high commit rate under heavy contention as an engine property: every completion comes from retrying, with up to 79.4% of attempts wasted at 64 threads, and no completion in usable time at 1,000 (section 6).
  • Starvation prevented by aging: contradicted, one transaction aborted 1,980 times (Part 3, section 7).
  • Deadlocks eliminated: detected and broken, thousands of times, not prevented.

11. What's left to fix

  1. The late-release race: pass the aborted attempt to releaseAllLocks and skip it if the registry holds a newer object (Part 3, section 6). This is the only known serializability bug.
  2. Flush the benchmark CSV after every run, so a killed sweep keeps its rows.
  3. Starvation: keep each transaction's first-attempt timestamp across restarts and never abort the oldest (wound-wait style), or take age into account when granting locks.
  4. Lock-manager scaling (curve A): per-resource condition variables or wait queues instead of one notify_all, and a striped lock table instead of one global mutex.
  5. Take file I/O out of the critical path in the text harness (RAG snapshots off by default, buffered logging).
  6. Durability, if ACID is a goal: a write-ahead log with undo/redo records, flushed at commit.
  7. Measurement gaps: re-run the text harness on the fixed engine, and add C at 128–256 threads with a time limit per run.
